The Salt Road

Nobody in Linh Dien remembered who had first called it the salt road, though everyone agreed it had never carried much salt. It ran north out of the village between two rows of drowned mangroves, and in the dry months the mud on either side cracked into plates the colour of old bread.1 Her grandmother had walked it every spring with a basket on her head and a story for each bend, and Mai had grown up believing that a road was only a long way of telling something.

By the time the ferry horn sounded across the flats she had already counted the posts twice. Forty-one, the same as yesterday, and the same as the year the floods came. The ferryman, who had once been a schoolteacher, liked to say that the village was the only place in the delta where the water kept better time than the clocks.

ENGINEERING NOTES
What a year of weekly releases taught us
HMHoa Mai·12 Nov 2026·8 min read

When we moved from monthly to weekly releases last autumn, the first thing that changed was not the code. It was the Fridays. They became calmer than they had been in years, and nobody could quite say why until we counted the incidents.

Smaller batches, calmer Fridays

Release train, week 14: the board that replaced our monthly checklist.

A monthly release carried about sixty tickets. A weekly one carries fourteen. When something breaks, the list of suspects is short enough to read out loud in a stand-up, and the rollback is a single revert instead of an archaeology project.

We kept the same checks, the same reviewers and the same board. What went away was the release freeze, the Thursday night spreadsheet and the quiet dread.

Nobody is afraid of a release that is only forty lines long.
Tuan Vo, on the first Friday we shipped before lunch

What the numbers said

Over the year we shipped 51 releases. Rollbacks fell from nine to two, and the median time from merge to production dropped to under four hours.

CHAPTER THREE
The Salt Road

Nobody in Linh Dien remembered who had first called it the salt road, though everyone agreed it had never carried much salt. It ran north out of the village between two rows of drowned mangroves, and in the dry months the mud on either side cracked into plates the colour of old bread.1 Her grandmother had walked it every spring with a basket on her head and a story for each bend, and Mai had grown up believing that a road was only a long way of telling something.

By the time the ferry horn sounded across the flats she had already counted the posts twice. Forty-one, the same as yesterday, and the same as the year the floods came. The ferryman, who had once been a schoolteacher, liked to say that the village was the only place in the delta where the water kept better time than the clocks.

ENGINEERING NOTES
What a year of weekly releases taught us
HMHoa Mai·12 Nov 2026·8 min read

When we moved from monthly to weekly releases last autumn, the first thing that changed was not the code. It was the Fridays. They became calmer than they had been in years, and nobody could quite say why until we counted the incidents.

Smaller batches, calmer Fridays

Release train, week 14: the board that replaced our monthly checklist.

A monthly release carried about sixty tickets. A weekly one carries fourteen. When something breaks, the list of suspects is short enough to read out loud in a stand-up, and the rollback is a single revert instead of an archaeology project.

We kept the same checks, the same reviewers and the same board. What went away was the release freeze, the Thursday night spreadsheet and the quiet dread.

Nobody is afraid of a release that is only forty lines long.
Tuan Vo, on the first Friday we shipped before lunch

What the numbers said

Over the year we shipped 51 releases. Rollbacks fell from nine to two, and the median time from merge to production dropped to under four hours.

Three rules we kept

Every change ships behind a flag, every flag has an owner, and every owner has a date. When the date passes, the flag goes. It sounds strict and it is, which is why it works.

What we would change

We would add the release notes to the pull request template instead of writing them on Friday. We would also give the on-call person a name on the board, not just a rota.
