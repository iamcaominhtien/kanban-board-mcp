What a shared board taught us about passing work to a team

Six months of tickets, reports and one very patient reviewer.

ANAn Nguyen·Oct 5, 2026·6 min read

When we first let an AI team pick up a ticket, we expected the hard part to be the code. It was the briefing. A ticket that a colleague could finish from a hallway conversation turned out to need a paragraph of context, a link to the right page and a clear sense of done.

So we started writing tickets for a stranger. Each one names the goal, points to the Rate limiting page it belongs to, and lists what a reviewer will check. People noticed first: the stranger turned out to be us, three weeks later.

A ticket is a promise written for a stranger. The stranger is sometimes a team of models.An Nguyen, on the retro

Write the ticket for a stranger

A good brief fits on one screen. We settled on three short parts and stopped adding fields:

  • Goal: one sentence, in the language of the product, not the code.
  • Where: the pages and tickets that already explain the area.
  • Done: the three checks a reviewer will run.

Tracked in KAN-12, the first brief written this way shipped a week early.

Read the report, not the log

The log tells you what happened. The report tells you what the team decided and what it wants you to look at. We read the second and open the first only when something smells.

The Harbour Lights · Book one
Chapter three

The Long Tide

◆

By the time the tide turned, Mara had counted forty-one lamps along the harbour wall and lost count of the boats. The ferry from Cát Bà would not run again until morning,1 and the keeper’s cottage, which the letter had promised would be unlocked, stood dark against the hill.

She set her case down on the stones and listened. Somewhere beyond the breakwater a bell was ringing the way bells do when nobody is pulling the rope: slow, then slower, as though the sea had taken over the work.2 She thought of her mother’s handwriting, which had never once leaned the same way twice.

“You are the niece,” said a voice behind her. It was not a question, and it did not sound unkind. An old man stood where the lamplight gave out, a coil of net over one shoulder and a lantern he had not bothered to light.

“I was told the door would be open,” Mara said.

“The door is always open,” he said. “It is the house that is difficult.”

1The last ferry left at 6:40 in winter and 7:15 in summer; the timetable on the pier was never updated.
2Sailors call it a ghost toll. The bell is moved by the swell, not by hands.

Short story

The Night Clerk

TVTuan Vo·Sep 18, 2026·9 min read

The Hotel Aurora had forty rooms and, on most nights, three guests. Lan liked it that way. At two in the morning the lobby clock was the loudest thing in the building, and she had long ago stopped hearing it, the way you stop hearing your own name.

She kept a ledger in pencil. Names, room numbers, and in the margin a word or two she never showed anyone: snores, widow, pays in coins. Management had asked, twice, for the ledger to go on a screen. Lan had said she would think about it, and that had been two winters ago.

The radiator under the desk knocked three times, as it did when the boiler remembered itself. She wrote the hour, drew a line under it, and waited for nothing in particular.

* * *

On Thursday the man in room twelve came down in his socks and asked whether it was possible to be charged for a dream.

“Depends on the dream,” Lan said, and reached for the pencil.
