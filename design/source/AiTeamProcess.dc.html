Screen — v1 proposal

An AI team with a process — packs, skills, workflows and who holds the ball

Wave 2 of the AI plan (docs/specs/ai-claude-code-plan.md). Today the AIs pass work to each other by hand, a ticket has one assignee box and no notion of a process, and nothing records who did which part. This board gives the team its shape: skills and plugins you can install and switch per AI, role packs you can export, a repository scan that proposes a team for a new project, workflows a ticket follows with a role on every step, review at a depth that suits the ticket, an AI that settles disagreements before you are asked, evidence that a test really went red before it went green, a way to interrupt a run with a sentence, and numbers per workflow template rather than per AI.
A. What this board decides
Every row is already decided in the plan document; the grey chip says which decision. D1–D25 are the decisions in section 2 of docs/specs/ai-claude-code-plan.md.
The question
The decision
Why, and where it comes from
How does a ticket get done?
It follows a workflow: a few named steps, each with a role. Triage picks the template, you change it. Handing work straight to one AI stays for odd jobs.
There is no notion of a process today, so the AIs invent one each time. D8
How many templates?
Four ready-made — Quick fix, Standard feature, Feature with a UI, Research — editable, and you can write your own.
Few steps, split only where the context has to change; the checker is independent and gets a diff, not a transcript. D9
Who is the assignee now?
Whoever is holding the ball. It changes with the step, and it never erases who did the steps before.
A hand-off overwrites the assignee today, so the ticket forgets its own history. D14
When do you have to look?
Twice: the design step, and the last one. Everything between is the AIs’ business.
Any more and the process is not saving you anything. D10
How many times may it go round?
As many as it takes. It stops on no progress, a board fault, or a per-ticket quota cap you turned on.
A fixed limit cuts off work that was about to finish. No progress is the honest signal. D11
Who settles a disagreement?
A coordinator AI, independent of both, on Opus. It picks a side, or splits the ticket, or says it cannot — then you.
You should not be the first reader of an argument two AIs could have settled. D12 D24
Is everything reviewed?
Yes. The depth changes: Light, Standard or Deep, chosen by Triage from the size and the risk, changed by you.
Reviewing a typo as hard as a migration wastes the window that the migration needed. D13
What is a role pack?
Everything that makes an AI that role: persona, model and effort, tools, permissions and ceiling, where it may write, what it must hand in, limits. Exportable.
Today the four presets are a persona and a few numbers, and TDD is a sentence in prose. D15
Where do skills and plugins come from?
A git URL, the Claude Code marketplace, or a folder on your machine. No approval step — whoever installs it carries the risk.
The board still records the source and the commit, shows what changed on an update, and has one button back. D1
How does an AI get them?
Through CLI flags at the start of each run, not by writing into the repository.
--plugin-dir, --agents, --settings, --append-system-prompt-file. Needs S1–S4 first. D5
How does a new project get a team?
The board reads the repository, a light model proposes a team and the commands, one trial run proves them, you confirm.
Nothing is created from the scan alone, and the trial is what separates a guess from a fact. D19
Can you interrupt a run?
Yes — a sentence to the AI that is working, and a chat with the coordinator that assigns work itself.
The first needs --input-format stream-json, which is still an experiment (S6). D20

Everything here travels together when the pack is exported.
Who it isA persona: writes the failing test first, keeps the change as small as the criteria allow, never reformats a file it did not touch.
Model and effortSonnet at medium. Opus at high only on the second attempt at the same step, never on the first.
ToolsThe project’s default plugins, plus tdd. Switchable per member. Section B
Permissions and ceilingThe developer template; it may move a ticket as far as Review and no further. Changing an assignee needs approval; deleting a ticket is never allowed.
Where it may writeThe worktree of its own ticket. Nothing outside it, and nothing in the main checkout.
What it must hand inA run where the test failed, and a later run where it passed — both recorded, not claimed in prose. This is the part a persona cannot enforce.

What it is notNot the reviewer of its own work, and not allowed to change an acceptance criterion — only to propose one.
Which member does itThe step names the role. With two Devs on the project, it goes to the one that is free, and when both are free to the one that already worked on this ticket — so a ticket keeps the same Dev across its steps. With none, see the state in section M.

The team always covers the workflow
  • The roles suggested are exactly the ones the suggested workflow has steps for, plus any the repository argues for.
  • Otherwise the first ticket would stop at a step with nobody to do it — the state in section M, on day one.
  • Removing a role here changes the suggested workflow too, and the screen says which steps would go.
  • Triage is always in: it is what puts a workflow on a new ticket in the first place.
Why the trial run matters
  • A command read out of a README is a guess. A command that just ran is a fact, and the difference costs two minutes once.
  • It also warms the prepare step, so the first real ticket is not the one that discovers npm ci takes four minutes.
  • A failing command is shown with its output. The wizard does not go on pretending.
  • The scratch worktree is removed afterwards, whatever happened.
When it runs again
  • When the CI workflow changes a command, or a package manager appears that was not there before.
  • It never changes anything by itself: it offers the same screen with what moved highlighted.
  • On a project that already has a team, only the parts you have not touched are re-suggested.
  • The scan is also what the Suggested for a role tab of Skills & plugins reads.

What you can change about a template
  • Add, remove and reorder steps; rename them.
  • Who does each step, what it must hand in, what it is given, and which model.

G. Review at a depth that fits, and the one design you approve
Every ticket is reviewed by an AI. What changes is how hard. The other thing that changes is where you are asked to look: twice in a workflow with a UI, once in the others.
LightHaiku
Chosen when  Under ~80 lines changed, no label marked risky, a bug fix or a chore.
Reads  The diff and the acceptance criteria. Nothing else.
A few seconds. Catches the obvious: a missing case, a stray log line, a criterion not met.
StandardSonnet
Chosen when  The default. Anything that is neither tiny nor risky.
Reads  The diff, the criteria, the files the diff touches, and the hand-over note.
A minute or two. Reads the change in the context of the code around it.
DeepOpus · high
Chosen when  A migration, anything touching money or permissions, a label you marked risky, or a ticket already sent back twice.
Reads  All of the above, plus whoever calls the changed code, and it may run the tests itself.
The most expensive thing in the workflow, on the tickets where being wrong costs most.

Why review is never off
  • An AI reviewer is cheap compared with the ticket going out wrong, and it is the only reader that sees every change.
  • What is tunable is the depth, not whether it happens. Triage picks it from the size, the labels and the kind of ticket; you change it on the ticket.
  • A ticket sent back twice moves up a level by itself and says so — but only when Triage chose the level. A level you set by hand is never overridden; the ticket says the depth would have gone up instead.
  • The reviewer gets the diff and the criteria, never the transcript: a reader who watched the work being done cannot judge it fresh.
  • The four built-in templates stop for you exactly twice — the design and the last step. The editor warns when a template you write adds a third, and the accept it anyway buttons in section I belong to that last step, with everything else you are approving, rather than forming a stop of their own. How it works
Why
What triggers it
The same two AIs pass a ticket back a third time and the diff barely moved, or the reviewer repeats an objection it already made.
Round count alone is not it: a ticket genuinely improving on round four should not be stopped. D11
Who arbitrates
A coordinator chosen in the project settings — a PM member, independent of both, on Opus at high effort.
It is not one of the two arguing, and it does not hold the ticket. D12 D24
What it is given
The diff, the acceptance criteria and both sides’ comments. Neither transcript.
The same rule as the reviewer: judge the work, not the process that made it.
What it may decide
Side with one, or split the ticket. It may propose a change to an acceptance criterion; it may not make one.
A changed criterion is marked, and you see the mark at the last step.
When it reaches you
Only when the coordinator declines or deadlocks — then it is a row in Needs you with the verdict attached.
The row says the coordinator saw it first, so you know this is not round one. Section Needs you, wave 1
What is recorded
The arbitration is a run like any other: it is in the history, it is pinned, and the ticket links to it.
So “why was it decided that way” is answerable later.
If the ruling does not settle it
A ticket that comes back a second time after a ruling goes to you, with the ruling attached — not to the coordinator again.
The coordinator has had its turn; a second pass there is the same argument at Opus prices. D12
Who outranks whom
You > Board AI > the coordinator > the members. An instruction from the Board AI, which speaks for you, overrides a ruling, and the override is recorded on the ticket next to it.
The coordinator is a member: it reaches you through the Board AI and the notification, never by holding the ticket. D24
Triage is not the coordinator
Triage is its own pack on Haiku; the coordinator is a PM on Opus at high effort.
Keeping them apart is what lets the coordinator stay independent of the ticket it is judging.

How a pair is matched
  • Same test command, same test path, same ticket, the red one before the green one, and no change to the test file between them.
  • Red for the right reason. The named test must fail on an assertion. A collection error, an import failure, a syntax error or a missing file means the suite never ran that test, so it proves nothing and the pair is refused.
  • A test changed between the two runs breaks the pair, and says so: moving the goalposts is the thing this is meant to catch.
  • It works without any new recording. Wave 1 already keeps the command, the exit code and the output of every step.
  • Only steps whose pack asks for it are checked. A Designer step has no tests and is never marked unproven.
What it is not
  • Not a coverage number. Whether the test is any good is the reviewer’s job.
  • Not a gate you cannot pass: accepting without it is one button, and it is recorded on the ticket.
  • Not retroactive. Tickets finished before this exists are not marked unproven.
  • Not on by default for every pack — only Dev (TDD) asks for it.

M. The states these screens have to have
The ones that decide whether a process feels like help or like paperwork.
A ticket with no workflow
Perfectly normal. The strip is replaced by one line: “No workflow — assigned to Dev An”, with an optional Reviewer field and a button to put a workflow on it.
The workflow changed mid-ticket
The strip says which steps were dropped and which were added, and the ones already done keep their marks. Nobody re-does a finished step.
A step has no member for its role
The step says “no Designer on this project” and offers to make one from the pack, or to skip the step for this ticket. It does not sit there silently.
The pack’s obligation is unmet
The step is not done and says what is missing, with one button to accept it anyway. Recorded on the ticket either way.
A plugin is not installed yet
A member whose pack wants a plugin the project has not installed runs without it, and the member page says which one and offers to install it.
The scan found nothing
A repository with no manifest and no CI: the wizard says what it could not find and asks for the three commands by hand, instead of inventing them.
The coordinator is unset
Disagreements go straight to you, and the settings row says that is what is happening. No silent fallback.
A template is in use
Editing one asks what to do with the tickets already on it: let them finish on the old shape, or move them over. Never decided for you.
Too few tickets to judge
A template with fewer than ten tickets shows its numbers with no verdict, the same rule the AI tiles already use.

Decided before building
  • A step is a role, not a person. Whichever member holds that role picks it up, so a workflow survives a member being removed.
  • The assignee never erases history. It stays one box, and the step rows keep who did what.
  • You are asked twice at most: the design, and the last step.
  • Review always happens; only the depth changes. The reviewer never sees the transcript.
  • No round limit. It stops on no progress, a board fault, or a cap you turned on — never on a count alone.
  • A coordinator tries before you are asked, and may propose a change to a criterion but never make one. It is a PM member, independent of both sides and of Triage; the Board AI outranks it and you outrank both.
  • A ruling gets one chance. A ticket that comes back after one goes to you, with the ruling attached.
  • A machine check runs before any AI review and is a step of its own, so a reviewer is never spent on a change that does not build.
  • A step names a role, not a member, and resolves to whoever is free — preferring the one that already worked on this ticket.
  • Installing from the internet has no approval step, and the board says plainly what that means.
  • Nothing is written into the repository to configure a run: it all goes through CLI flags.
  • Templates are versioned, so measurements stay comparable and a running ticket is not rewritten.
  • The scan creates nothing until you press Create, and it proves its commands by running them once.
What the build still has to settle
  • Whether plugins load at all under -p (S1), whether their hooks run (S3), whether --resume survives a changed plugin list (S2), and what ten plugins do to start-up time (S4). The whole of section B assumes yes; if S1 fails, skills stay as they are and only the catalogue and the usage count survive.
  • Sending a sentence to a running AI (S6). Section J draws all three outcomes; only the second one works without it.
  • How much of a workflow Triage can pick well. The design lets you change every choice it makes. If it turns out to be right nine times in ten, the change-it affordances can get quieter; if not, Triage proposes instead of deciding.
  • Whether “no progress” can be detected from the diff alone. Comparing two diffs is easy; knowing that a reworded objection is the same objection needs a model. The first version can be strict and let you lower it.
  • How visible the override has to be. D24 settles the order — you > Board AI > the coordinator > the members — and section H records a Board AI instruction that overrides a ruling next to the ruling itself. Whether that is readable enough on a busy ticket is a build question, not a design one.
  • Whether a User tester is useful without a person watching. It is the one pack in this board nobody has run yet.
